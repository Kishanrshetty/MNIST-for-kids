      How Neural Numbers works

      The same three letters, T, P and E, explain the whole thing.

      T

      The task: reading messy handwriting

      Handwriting is wobbly. Nobody writes a 7 the same way twice, and one slightly longer line can turn a 1 into a 7 or a 3 into an 8. Writing exact rules for every possible shape is nearly impossible, so instead we let the computer learn from examples, the way you did.

      Before the AI looks, your drawing is tidied up: cropped, shrunk to fit a 20 × 20 box and centred on a 28 × 28 grid. That makes 784 dots, each a number from 0 (no ink) to 1 (full ink). Ten answer neurons, one for each digit, show how sure the AI is.

      Did you know?

      People used to make the task easier for computers. A barcode is only black and white stripes, and the Palm Pilot handhelds of the late 1990s made people learn a special simplified alphabet called Graffiti.

      P

      The performance: a fair score

      To measure Performance we use drawings the brain has never studied. The score is simple: numbers read right ÷ numbers tried. Your 10-number test gives a quick score. The big exam uses 10,000 drawings that were kept out of training on purpose.

      Why new drawings? A brain tested on pictures it already studied could look clever just by remembering them, like acing a quiz you have seen the answers to.

      Connect it

      Machines that sort letters by postcode and read the amounts on bank cheques were some of the first real jobs for this kind of AI. Their makers judged them the same way: the share of new numbers read correctly.

      E

      The experience: labelled examples

      Each example is a picture plus the right answer, written by a person. The brain guesses, checks the answer, then nudges its 52,544 connections a tiny bit so the right answer gets stronger next time. Repeat that thousands of times.

      Our examples come from MNIST, a collection of 70,000 handwritten digits by US Census Bureau staff and American high-school students: 60,000 to learn from and 10,000 for the exam. When you press No, wrong and give the right number, you add one more piece of Experience yourself.

      Did you know?

      The brain was never told what a 4 looks like. Draw a plus sign: it often says 4, because it learned the shape from crossing lines, not from a rule.
